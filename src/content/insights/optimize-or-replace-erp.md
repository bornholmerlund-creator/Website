Replacing an ERP is an 18-month, often seven-figure decision. Before committing to one, it's worth asking whether the system is genuinely the problem — or whether it was simply never configured, integrated and adopted properly. In our experience, it's the latter more often than vendors selling replacements would have you believe.

## Five questions to ask before you sign

**1. Are the painful problems about capability, or configuration?**
"It can't do X" is sometimes true and often means "it was never set up to do X." Establish which before you spend.

**2. How much of what you paid for is switched on?**
Many mid-sized businesses run a fraction of their ERP's modules. Unused capability you already own is the cheapest improvement available.

**3. Is the pain really an integration gap?**
Re-keying, disagreeing stock figures and spreadsheet reconciliation are usually symptoms of disconnected systems, not a failed core — and integration is far cheaper than replacement.

**4. Is the data the actual problem?**
If reports can't be trusted, the cause is often data quality and governance, which a new system will inherit unless you fix it first. Replacement doesn't clean your data; it migrates the mess.

**5. What does an honest, vendor-neutral assessment say?**
The firm pitching the replacement is not the firm to answer questions one through four. Get a read from someone with no licence commission at stake.

## The cheaper path is often the right one

A structured health check — two to four weeks — usually pays for itself many times over, either by avoiding an unnecessary replacement or by de-risking one that genuinely is needed. Replacement is sometimes the right call. It should never be the default one.
